Who is holdingthe fire

Three kinds of act, and they ask for different things. Movement acts build character from silhouette. Voice acts name it out loud. Music acts let the loop do the structuring. One character per act, no borrowing between sections — a face that appears twice stops being a character.

The characters you already have

Acts that already exist, split by type, with their choreography or lyrics. Noted here for what they own, so the new characters do not take it.

Voice

The boxA cardboard box wanders into a fire circus, very slowly
  • The object A big box built from several boxes, googly eyes on the top one, you inside it. Large enough to read from the back of the pitch
  • Movement Slow travel across the stage. Inches at a time, never hurried, never explained. The slowness is half the comedy
  • Voice A curious newcomer. Warm, chatty, excited, hums to itself, and thinks constantly about the fact that it is made of cardboard
  • Alternate voice The little tyrant: tiny, cute, and giving orders. It never asks, and the joke is how quickly the audience obeys. “Clap now!” Proposed: “Louder. The box cannot hear you.” “Not you. You clapped wrong.” “Did I say stop?” “You. Stand up. … Sit down. The box was only checking.” “Good. The box is pleased.” Keep it pleased easily, so it stays cute.
  • Entrance Out through the door, humming, then down the stairs — the audience learns how hard it is to be a box before a word of the act
  • The stake Everything on this bill can kill it. That is real jeopardy, and it is funny precisely because it is real
  • “Fire is not for the cardboard-hearted.”
  • Drifts slightly too close to a lit prop, panics, retreats at exactly the same unhurried speed
  • Reviews the previous act. Generous about the technique, concerned about the ventilation
  • Asks the audience to look after it. Names one person as responsible
  • The other performers walk past and ignore it completely
  • Sheds a box as it travels. Does not acknowledge this
  • Ending: it either makes it across, or it stops one metre short and calls that a success

The tarot readerA fortune teller who will let you draw again
  • Prop A deck, oversized so the cards read from the back
  • Form One audience member at a time, direct address, no build-up
  • Engine Total confidence and no hedging. Readings are absurdly literal and about four words long
  • “Who wants a card?”
  • Three kids. Happy life. Next.
  • Bad news. Better pick another card.
  • The same card twice in a row, read completely differently
  • One person’s card you refuse to read. You just look at them and move on
  • A card that is obviously not a tarot card at all
  • Come back to someone later in the show with an update on their reading

Sketch

[Comes out with the deck. Oversized cards. No introduction, no build-up.]

Who wants a card?

[Takes the first hand up. Fans the deck. They draw.]

[Turns the card round so the back row can see it. Looks at it for one second.]

Three kids. Happy life. Next.

[Straight to the next person. They draw.]

Bad news. Better pick another card.

[They draw again. Brighten immediately.]

Much better. Well done.

[Third person draws — the same card as the first.]

Completely different meaning.

[Fourth person draws. Look at the card. Look at them. Say nothing. Move on.]

[Fifth card is obviously not a tarot card — a bank card, an Uno card, a photo of the coconut.]

Oh. This one is rare.

[Give it to them to keep. Walk off still reading cards for nobody.]

Total confidence, four words, never explain. The speed is the joke — the moment you start interpreting, it stops being funny. If you spot someone later in the night, give them an update on their reading as if you have been thinking about it since.

The reroll is the whole joke. A fortune teller who lets you draw again has no stakes, which is exactly what makes it funny — and it means nobody in the audience ever has to sit with a bad prediction.

Keep readings absurd rather than cruel, and never predict anything real about a real person’s actual life. Four words, move on. The moment a reading lands too close, the room stops laughing and starts feeling for them.

Movement & fire acts

No words. Everything the audience learns comes from silhouette, tempo and your relationship to the flame. These are the acts that work at the back of a dark field.

Fear of fireThe flame has its own will and it is coming for you
  • Song Nina Simone — Sinnerman. Also Art of Noise — Peter Gunn, Screamin’ Jay Hawkins — I Put a Spell on You
  • Prop Contact staff — it already looks alive
  • Tempo Flinch and freeze, never smooth until the end
  • States Terror → tolerance → one accidental moment of ease → panic → surrender. Each one is held right through the technique, not dropped for it
  • Changes The switch from one state to the next happens between moves, never inside one. Five or six junctions is the whole act
  • Lights it, then holds it at arm's length like a dead rat
  • Puts it down and it follows — always behind you when you turn
  • Blows on it to put it out. Repeatedly. Politely
  • A perfect isolation while looking away, then terror on noticing
  • It rolls across the shoulders and you go rigid, holding breath
  • Ending: eyes closed, full rolling sequence, fear gone

The technique is not the story. A clean roll says nothing by itself — what the audience reads is the state you are carrying while you do it, and what changes when you come out of it. Pick the emotion first and let the move happen inside it.

Which makes the act a list of junctions: the flinch before picking it up, the reaction after a roll goes well, the pause where he decides to try again, the moment he stops negotiating with it. Write those. The technique between them only has to be good enough not to intrude.

Worship of fireThe flame is sacred and you are its servant
  • Song Dead Can Dance — The Host of Seraphim. Also Arvo Pärt — Spiegel im Spiegel, Hildegard von Bingen — chant
  • Prop Dragon staff — overhead it is a halo, a sun disc
  • Tempo Slow throughout, long stillness between phrases
  • Sound Chant or invented liturgy. Texture, not speech — nonsense language keeps it ritual, real words invite decoding
  • Enter on knees, staff carried flat as an offering
  • Overhead halo spin, held a beat longer than comfortable
  • Procession walk, tiny steps, the staff never stops turning
  • Bow to the flame before every phrase
  • Others try to touch it and you block them
  • Ending: lay it down still burning, back away, never turn away

Play this one beautiful, not comic. It is the quiet centre that makes the manic acts land harder.

The hip hop dancerNot a breakdancer, and the last to find out
  • Song The Offspring — Pretty Fly (For a White Guy). Also Vanilla Ice — Ice Ice Baby, Beastie Boys — Sabotage
  • The point Not being a breakdancer is the character, not a limitation. The song already says he is pretty fly for a white guy — it does the mocking so you never have to
  • Commitment Absolute. He believes every second of it. The moment he knows he is bad, there is no act
  • Tempo Bursts. Big attempt, recovery, look around, bigger attempt
  • One move he is genuinely good at, used far too many times
  • Attempts something clearly beyond him, fails, and immediately presents it as intentional
  • Checks whether anyone saw. Somebody always saw
  • Tries to make the staff part of the routine. The staff refuses
  • Holds a freeze far too long, waiting for a reaction that is not coming
  • Gets genuinely out of breath and keeps going
  • Ending: one thing that actually is impressive, at the exact moment nobody is looking

Keep him clear of the lover. The lover wants to be desired and is about eighty per cent succeeding. This one wants to be respected and is at zero, and has not noticed. Smooth versus straining — if both are in the show, that difference has to be visible in the first five seconds.

One thing to be deliberate about: the joke is on him, never on the form. Do the moves with real commitment and let the failure be his. Played the other way — sloppy, winking, mugging at hip hop itself — it curdles fast and the room feels it.

Useful structurally too. Movement is heavy on slow and strange right now, and this is one of the few fast, loud acts in the section.

Split personalityTwo people, one body, one staff, no agreement
  • Song Danger Mouse & Daniele Luppi — Two Against One (feat. Jack White). Also Rome — rest of the album, Dueling Banjos
  • What it sets Slow and prowling rather than frantic. It pushes the act toward menace and swagger, which is closer to the polite argument below than a comic squabble would be
  • Prop Contact staff — the handoff across the body is already a change of owner
  • Split Left and right, not front and back. Left high, sharp, fast; right low, slack, soft
  • The staff freezes mid-transfer and the two halves fight for it
  • One side walks offstage, the other drags it back
  • Left hand does a trick, right hand applauds
  • Steal the staff from your own hand behind your back
  • Whisper to your own shoulder
  • Ending: both sides sync for one clean sequence, then break again

Resist playing this as straightforward madness. A manic character wants out — one intention, struggling. The split character has two intentions and only wants to win the argument. Calmer and more absurd: a polite disagreement that escalates.

The machineThe impulse is internal — and it is malfunctioning
  • Song Daft Punk — Harder, Better, Faster, Stronger. Also Kraftwerk — The Robots, Aphex Twin — Come to Daddy
  • Tempo Metronomic, never breaks, even when wrong
  • Prop Either staff. The dragon staff reads more mechanical — a wheel turning in a body
  • Same phrase three times, fourth time it goes wrong, reboot
  • Glitch the loop and the body glitches with it
  • Stuck on one isolation, repeating, until someone hits you
  • Powers down mid-move and holds an impossible position
  • Speed creeps up until the movement outruns the body

The puppetThe impulse is external — someone else is pulling
  • Song Nino Rota — La Strada. Also Satie — Gymnopédie No. 1, The Tiger Lillies
  • Body Limbs lead, the body follows and hangs between impulses
  • Key Go slack the instant nothing pulls you. The gap is the whole character
  • Best version A visible puppeteer — the lover discovering they can control you
  • A string lifts the wrist and the staff comes with it
  • Dropped mid-phrase, collapses, the staff keeps rolling alone
  • Fights the strings for two seconds, loses
  • The janitor’s broom becomes the puppeteer

Keep it clearly apart from the machine directly above. Machine repeats and resets, puppet hangs and drops. Machine is funny, puppet is sad.

The currentFire behaving like water, and not much interested in you
  • Song Osamu Kitajima — Benzaiten, God of Music & Water. Also Kitarō — Koi, Hiroshi Yoshimura — Music for Nine Postcards
  • Prop Contact staff — rolls are the only unbroken line you own. Dragon staff if the disc reads as a ripple rather than a halo
  • The one rule Never stop. No accents, no held positions, no phrase that begins or ends
  • Pathway Always travelling. Spirals, figure eights, low circular routes. Never rooted to one spot
  • Enter already in motion. The audience should feel they caught a piece of something ongoing
  • Weight shifts that never settle — the body is carried, not driven
  • The staff changes level continuously; nothing is ever presented
  • No acknowledgment of the audience at any point, and no hostility either. They are a riverbank
  • Ending: leave still moving. Do not finish, just go out of sight

Keep it clear of the worship act. Worship is built on stillness, punctuation and service — this has none of the three. Worship bows to the flame; the current is indifferent to it. If both acts are in the same show, put something loud between them.

Play water, not a country. The music carries its own culture perfectly well, and the moment the act reaches for costume or gesture shorthand it stops being elemental and starts being decoration.

The creatureThe animal underneath, and it does not know it is being watched
  • Song Glass Animals — Toes. Also Suuns — Up Past the Nursery, Colin Stetson — New History Warfare Vol. 3
  • Body No upright walking. Low, on all fours or close to it. The staff flat on the back is a spine, a shell, a ridge — never a prop being held
  • Material Distance. Approach, retreat, approach closer. A whole act can be built from nothing but how near you get
  • Sound Breath under the track. Audible, wrong, not a human rhythm
  • Why this track It has gaps in it. The groove keeps the creature watchable rather than only uncomfortable, and the spaces are where the stillness and the charge live
  • Long watchful stillness, then one burst. The stillness is the main event — the charge only works if they relaxed first
  • Staff on the back, rush the audience, stop at the last moment
  • No acknowledgment of applause, no eye contact for their benefit. It looks at them the way an animal does — assessing, not entertaining
  • Investigates a person, loses interest, moves on

Keep it on the body, not the idea. The moment it becomes a concept about the self it goes vague and earnest. Played as an actual animal it stays unnerving and funny at once.

Safety and placement. Do the close work with the staff unlit or burnt down and keep the lit sequences at distance. Put it late in the running order — it needs the audience comfortable before you break that. It is the only genuinely dangerous act on the bill, which is exactly why it earns its place.

The janitorThe show is his workplace and everyone keeps making a mess of it
  • Song Satie — Gymnopédie No. 1. Also Vince Guaraldi — Linus and Lucy, Chet Baker
  • Prop Broom. Unlit, full light — rests the audience's eyes between burns and lets you play character where the face reads
  • Tempo Unhurried. Never rushes, never reacts, outlasts everything
  • Status Lowest in the show, which makes him the only one allowed to comment on it
  • States Invisible → noticed → reluctant → quietly the best performer on the bill → back to sweeping. The shifts land between phrases of broom work, never inside one
  • Sweeps ash between acts as if nothing unusual has happened
  • Tidies around a performer who is mid-act, without looking up
  • Tries to sweep the fire. Tries to sweep a spotlight
  • Finds a lit staff on the ground and treats it as litter
  • Alone at last, the broom becomes a dance partner
  • Ending: caught being brilliant, goes straight back to work

A broom is a servant's object, so it does half the character work before you move. This is also where the show's virtuosity can hide — the last person the audience expects to be good.

The grumpAnnoyed at how polished everything is — does it raw
  • Song Tom Waits — Goin’ Out West. Also The Stooges — I Wanna Be Your Dog
  • Tempo Heavy and blunt. No flourish, nothing presented, no finishing pose
  • Opening Walks on, looks at the clean stage, — tfu — spits on it

The spit is the whole idea in one second: this one is not here to be beautiful. Keep everything after it just as unpolished — scuffed, unhurried, never waiting for applause. The moment it gets tidy, the character is gone.

Practical. Spit away from the fire line and off the path people walk, or mime it. A wet patch on the stage is a slip.

Build for darkness

Facial expression does not survive a dark field. Silhouette does. Give each of these one distorted body line they never drop — a hunch, a lifted shoulder, turned-in feet — and one tempo that is clearly not anyone else's.

Voice acts

Standup, story, direct address. Voice removes the biggest limit fire imposes: you can name a character in the first ten seconds instead of building it through movement alone.

The confessorStandup that gets more personal than anyone asked for
  • Opens as ordinary crowd work, drifts somewhere too honest
  • The audience laughs, then stops, then laughs harder
  • Lights the staff at the point where the story gets unbearable and stops talking

The strongest use of voice you have, because it is the only one that cannot be done as a movement act.

The MC who won't stopOversells everything, including himself
  • Hypes each of the other acts far past what they deliver
  • Stays on too long, has to be physically removed
  • Keeps announcing his own act and never starting it

Useful structurally as well as comically — it stitches the show together and buys you time to change props.

The bad teacherExplains the technique, demonstrates it badly
  • Breaks down a move in earnest detail, then fails it
  • Blames the staff, the wind, the audience
  • Ends by doing it perfectly while mid-excuse, unaware

The narratorDescribes what is happening, sincerely, slightly wrong
  • Narrates another performer's act from the edge
  • Gets the emotion right and the facts wrong
  • Keeps narrating after the act has finished

Pairs with the chancer particularly well.

Separate the talking from the burning

Spoken word and fire compete for attention, and fire wins every time. Talk with the staff unlit or resting, then stop talking and burn. The talking builds the character, the fire pays it off. At full intensity together, each one flattens the other.

Voiceover acts

You are not the one being watched. Someone else is on stage and you are the voice over the top of it, which makes the commentary the act and the performers the material.

World Cup in SleepingElite competitive sleep, called by people who take it extremely seriously
  • On stage Two performers in pyjamas. They lie down, put on eye masks, and go to sleep
  • What it really is A micro fake sporting event of ginormous importance. An entire World Cup — rivalry, drama, ceremony, medal — compressed into about two minutes
  • The audience Not watching. They are the crowd, and each half of the room is assigned an athlete before it starts
  • The rule Total sincerity. The commentary never acknowledges that this is absurd. The moment it winks, it is over
  • Best with two voices Play-by-play and an expert summariser who disagrees with everything
  • Invented technique, delivered with complete authority — the side-curl, the full starfish, a textbook pillow commitment
  • Whisper throughout, like golf. Lose it completely at the climax
  • Snoring as a scoring event. The expert is unimpressed by the form
  • A wiggle at a crucial moment. Slow-motion replay of the wiggle
  • Fast-forward section — the performers speed up and the commentary keeps pace, barely
  • Sleepwalking: a clear rule violation. Consult the officials
  • Talk about their form this season, their coach, the injury they came back from
  • Photo finish, then one of them is World Champion. Medal ceremony, anthem, tears

Nothing on stage competes with the voice, so the commentary carries one hundred per cent of the act. That makes the performers easy to cast and the voice very hard — it needs the vocabulary and the conviction of real sports commentary, not a parody of it. Watch actual darts or golf coverage before writing it.

Compression is the joke. Two minutes, not five. Every ceremony a real tournament stretches over a month arrives at once — anthems, the match, the controversy, the photo finish, the medal, the tears. Long enough for the format to be recognised, short enough that it never becomes a sketch about sleeping.

Give them a side. This is the only act on the bill where the audience has a stake rather than a view. Section 1 and Section B are already set up earlier in the show — hand each of them an athlete and the earlier bit pays off as a genuine rivalry. Once somebody is cheering for a sleeping man in pyjamas they have stopped being an audience and become a crowd, and that energy carries into whatever follows.

It is also the only act on the bill with no fire and full light, which makes it a good reset between two burns and an easy piece to programme anywhere.

The format travels

Once the commentary voice exists it can be pointed at almost anything: the crew setting up a prop, the audience arriving, a queue, another act that has gone wrong. Worth building the voice as a reusable instrument rather than only for the sleeping piece.

Music acts, with movement

For a new character written for the loop. Disguise, the looping act that already exists, is in the cast at the top. Borrowing a character from another section would give two acts the same face.

What this section needs when you come back to it

A character who wants something a loop can deliver. The loop is a build, a repetition, a thing that can be broken on purpose — so the right character is one whose problem is accumulation or being stuck, not one who simply has a good song.

Layers going on is a build. A layer dropping out is a change of scene. Killing the loop dead is an ending. That is a five-minute shape for free, which no silent movement act gets — worth writing a character specifically to use it.

Finger puppets

A fourth format, and the only one on the bill where the audience has to lean in rather than back. Everything else in the show is big, hot and far away.

Puppet-landTwo centimetres tall, and enjoying it at your expense
  • Engine The puppets know perfectly well that nobody can see them, and they find it hilarious. The invisibility is the joke, and they are the ones making it
  • Status Inverted. Two tiny things looking down on a hundred people, with total contempt
  • Stage Your hands. No set, no lighting — wherever the hands are is where the world is
  • Carried by Voice. They are legible through sound long before anyone can make out a face
  • “Do you think they can see us out there, so far from the audience?” — “I hope they brought their microscopes. Mwehehe.”
  • An evil laugh far too big for the scale, which is the whole character in one sound
  • Plans for world domination, scoped to roughly the size of a shoebox
  • They discuss the fire performers with open contempt. Anyone can set fire to something
  • One puppet squints at the back row and reports what it can see. It is not flattering
  • Invite the audience to come closer, then mock them for obeying
  • They notice the crowd has stopped watching and pretend not to mind

Because the puppets do the mocking, the act works on any size pitch. The further back people are, the more the joke is on them, so a bad sightline is no longer a problem to solve. It also means the audience leaning in is a laugh rather than a failure.

Placement: straight after a fire act. Big, hot and dangerous, then two centimetres of pure malice. The scale drop is the effect, and it will not land anywhere else in the running order.

Keep it separate from the puppet in the movement acts. That one is silent, sad, and moved by someone else. These two talk, scheme and do the moving — different job entirely, so both can sit in the same show.

Still open. How many puppets, whether they return through the night as a running gag, and whether anything ever actually happens to them.

The visibility question, answered

Handing the scale problem to the puppets solves it completely. They are not small despite the audience — they are small at the audience. That means no staging fix is needed, the act costs nothing to tour, and the worse the sightline the better the joke gets.

The one thing it needs is volume. Everything visual is lost past the third row, so the voices have to carry the entire act. Worth testing at the back of the pitch before it goes in the show.

Interactive

Three tools in progress. None of them is an act yet, and the fastest way to make them work is to stop trying to make them one — attach each to a character that already stands up without it.

Circus game controllerQR code, phones, a voting system — audience decides
  • Status Works technically, does not yet work as an act
  • What it is missing Stakes. A vote is only interesting if the loser has to live with it
  • Attach it to The puppet. The audience becomes the puppeteer, which is that character’s entire premise and currently requires another performer
  • Vote on which act comes next, and the losing act never happens
  • Vote on whether he gets another attempt after a drop
  • Vote on which prop, then he has to do it with that one
  • Two options that are both bad. Announce the result as though it were their idea
  • One vote late in the night where the result is obviously ignored. “Interesting. No.”

Naming it a controller is the right instinct, because it tells the audience their input does something rather than being an opinion poll. Follow that through — if they control a performer, the performer must visibly obey, including when obeying is a bad idea.

The real obstacles. Getting a hundred people to scan costs a minute or more and you will lose a third of them, so it has to be set up once early and used several times across the night, never for a single gag. And outdoor pitches have bad data — assume the network fails and make sure every act it touches still runs without it.

A side benefit worth designing for: a field of lit phones is a visual. The crowd becomes a display, which is worth something on its own in the dark.

Circus looperLive looping built for acts rather than songs — WIP
  • Status In progress
  • Attach it to The machine, and to the empty music section. The loop pedal is the character — every layer is another system coming online

Layers going on is a build. A layer dropping out is a change of scene. Killing the loop dead is an ending. That is act structure that no silent movement piece gets for free, which is why the music section is worth filling with a character written specifically for it rather than one moved in from elsewhere.

What would make it circus-specific rather than a standard looper: a footswitch or trigger usable with hands full of staff, loops that can be dropped without looking, and a visible state so the audience can see the machine building. If they cannot see the layers accumulate, the structure is inaudible as structure.

Circus soundboardCued sound, with a generated-voice bundle
  • Status Bundled with a voice generator
  • Attach it to Puppet-land, the box, and the World Cup commentary

The generated voices solve a real problem rather than adding a gag: they give you a second performer. Puppet-land needs two voices arguing, the World Cup wants a summariser who disagrees with the commentator, and the box could be answered by something offstage. All of that currently needs a person.

The risk is that a canned voice cannot react, so anything it says has to be immune to timing. Give the generated voice the lines that work whenever they land, and keep every line that depends on the room for yourself.

Sound effects are worth more than voices for a fire show. The rewind, the snore, the buzzer, the anthem, the game-show sting — each one is a punchline you do not have to deliver.

Tech is a mechanism, never the act

Audience technology fails as a subject and succeeds as plumbing. If the act is “look, you can control this with your phone”, the novelty is spent in thirty seconds and the rest is admin. If the act is a puppet that obeys, and the phones are how the obeying happens, the tech disappears and the idea carries it.

The test for all three: unplug it. If the act collapses, it was not an act yet.

Staging notes

Where the story actually lives

A trick is not a story beat. However hard it is, a move on its own reads as display — the audience registers that it was impressive and nothing else. What they read as meaning is the emotional state you carry while doing it, and what changes when you come out of it.

So the state runs continuously, through the technique rather than around it. Nobody goes neutral to execute. The changes — the actual beats — happen in the joins: the moment before starting, the reaction after finishing, the decision to go again. A whole act is five or six of those.

Practically: write the junctions first and choose technique to fit them, rather than choreographing a sequence and looking for a character to put on top. It also means technique only has to be clean enough not to distract. Past that point, more difficulty buys spectacle, not story.

Keep one virtuoso, no more

Let a single character carry the real display of mastery — the janitor is the best candidate, because the reveal costs nothing and pays twice. Everyone else should be comic or strange. One skilful character reads as impressive; three reads as a demo. Skill is display, not content — more of it does not make a show say more.

Alternate the format

Two silent movement acts back to back blur together, and two talking acts in a row lose the room. Movement, then voice or music, then movement. The format change reads as a new act before the character even appears.

Cross-act bits

The janitor sweeps up behind the fear character, entirely unbothered. The lover works the front row and the puppet copies him badly. The chancer nearly sets himself alight and escapes it beautifully, twice. The creature watches the janitor sweep and does not move. The machine glitches and the split personality both halves blame each other.

Running order thought: alternate fast and slow as well as formats. The worship act and the puppet are what make the manic ones land.
